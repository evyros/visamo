# Certification and translation

## Where you married

A marriage in Israel, through the rabbinate or another religious court, is
already registered: its certificate needs no certification or translation. A
certificate from another country, or from an online marriage, is certified
and, if needed, translated (below). The marriage certificate's description
has the details.

## Apostille or consular legalization

A document from abroad has to be certified before Israel accepts it. Only
the country that issued it can certify it: it can't be done in Israel.

- From a country in the **Hague Apostille Convention** (the US, most of
  Europe and many others): an **apostille** from that country.
- From any other country: **consular legalization**, a longer chain of
  signatures (שרשרת חתימות): the authority that issued the document, then
  usually the country's ministry of justice and its foreign ministry, and last the
  Israeli consulate there.

Foreign documents follow the population authority's procedure 1.3.0001
(referenced by 5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).

- **Bring the originals.** Misrad Hapnim doesn't accept copies of these
  documents, only the originals with their certification. A copy is accepted
  only when the original can't be had, like an old birth certificate.
- **The originals have to be in Israel** for the first appointment, even if
  the foreign partner is still abroad: send them by courier to the Israeli
  partner in time. They're also needed for the translation.
- **Some documents come only as a file.** For Israel, the Philippines
  issues its PSA certificates (birth, marriage and death certificates, and
  the certificate of no marriage, CENOMAR) only as a file with an electronic
  apostille, sent by email. Printing them makes them invalid, so there's no
  paper original to ask for or send. Misrad Hapnim usually asks for
  originals on paper, so the clerk may push back on the files: explain that
  for Israel, the Philippines issues them only this way.
- **Ask for a paper document "for use abroad".** Many offices reject a
  document printed at home or downloaded from an online service, and many
  countries won't put an apostille on one. Ask for the printed original, with
  a handwritten signature or the official stamp, and say it's for use abroad
  or for an apostille.
- **Don't separate the apostille from the document.** It's usually attached
  to it, and separating them voids it.
- **Order a few copies of each document.** You may need another one later,
  for a renewal, a marriage, or a document that's lost.
- **Your everyday documents need no apostille.** A lease, bills, bank
  statements and similar documents from abroad are asked for to show your
  center of life or your relationship, not as official records of your
  status. They need no apostille or legalization. They only need a
  translation if they aren't in Hebrew, Arabic or English, and you can make
  it yourselves: translate them, or mark the important parts and explain
  them, so the clerk can follow. No notary is needed, unless the office asks
  for a notarized translation. The same goes for translating payslips and
  an insurance policy from abroad. Official documents and formal forms are different: a birth
  certificate, a police certificate or the landlord's affidavit needs a
  notarized translation, and an official document from abroad needs an
  apostille or legalization too.

## Translation

Documents are translated in one of two ways, depending on what they are:

- **Official documents and formal forms** (certificates, court decrees,
  police certificates, affidavits): a notarized translation (תרגום נוטריוני)
  into Hebrew, by a notary in Israel. Any other translation of them, even
  by a certified translator, will be rejected. The rest of this section is
  about them.
- **Personal/unofficial documents: your everyday documents and your own
  evidence** (a lease, bills, bank statements, payslips, an insurance
  policy, chats, emails, letters from friends): no notary, unlike official
  documents. If they aren't in Hebrew, Arabic or English, translate them
  yourselves, or mark the important parts and explain them, so the clerk
  can follow. A notarized translation is needed only if the office
  asks for one.

For official documents and formal forms, unlike personal/unofficial
documents such as bills, letters or bank statements:

- **By law, Misrad Hapnim (משרד הפנים) accepts only Hebrew and Arabic without a
  translation.** An official document in any other language needs a
  notarized translation into Hebrew. Personal/unofficial documents, like
  bills, letters or bank statements, don't: you can translate them
  yourselves.
  Procedure 1.3.0001 also lets a clerk accept a document in a language they
  fully understand. That's about the document itself, never its
  translation: a translation that isn't notarized will be rejected, whoever
  the clerk is.
- **English, in practice:** most clerks read English well enough to accept an
  English document without a translation, especially a short one. It depends
  on the clerk handling your file. If they don't accept it, they'll ask you
  to translate it and give you time to do it. So with English documents, be
  ready to translate.
  The AS/6 checklist itself says public documents need a notarized
  translation into Hebrew "except from Arabic and English" (מלבד מהשפות
  ערבית ואנגלית), which you can point a clerk to.
- **Every other language needs the notarized translation.** An official
  document in German, Russian, Spanish or any language other than Hebrew,
  Arabic or English needs a notarized translation into Hebrew. That's the
  requirement, not just the safest way. Personal/unofficial documents in
  those languages, like bills, letters or bank statements, still need no
  notary.
  What's accepted in practice for English is only about documents written in
  English: it doesn't make a translation into English enough.
- **What's translated:** the document, and the apostille or legalization on
  it too. An apostille that's in English, or that includes English, usually
  needs no translation.
- **Who translates:** a notary (נוטריון) who knows both languages and
  translates it themselves. A translation by a translator, even a certified
  or sworn one (מתרגם מוסמך), or by a translation agency, isn't a notarized
  translation: Misrad Hapnim will reject it. So will a translation someone
  else made, which a notary only approved. This doesn't depend on the clerk. If you already have one of these, have
  the official document translated again by a notary. Translate from the
  document's own language straight into Hebrew. For personal/unofficial
  documents, like bills, letters or bank statements, your own translation is
  enough.
- **Use a notary in Israel, not abroad.** We highly recommend it: Misrad
  Hapnim may reject a notarized translation made abroad. A translation done
  in Israel also needs no apostille: only the original document does. A
  notarized translation done abroad needs an apostille of its own, from the
  country where it was made, so the document has two apostilles: one on the
  document, one on the translation. Even then, it may not be accepted.
- **Into Hebrew, not English.** The notarized translation that's asked for is
  into Hebrew. A translation into English isn't what's asked for, and the
  clerk handling your file can refuse it.
- **If no notary in Israel knows the language,** Misrad Hapnim allows a double
  translation: first into a third language abroad (English, say), then from
  it into Hebrew in Israel.

## Affidavits

An affidavit (תצהיר) is a written statement that a person (the affiant,
המצהיר) makes of their own free will. They swear or affirm that it's true, in
front of someone the law authorizes to confirm it, who checks who they are
and warns them that they must tell the truth, and that giving false
information is punishable by law. Signing one is a legal act. In the partner
visa process, an affidavit proves a fact no official document shows: for
example, when the foreign partner's country doesn't issue a certain
certificate.

An affidavit is either **written freely**, as a list of numbered statements
with a section at the bottom for its confirmation (אישור), or **part of an
official form**, where you only fill in the missing details. Form AS/6 has
affidavits like this; they're explained with the form.

**Who can confirm one in Israel:**

- **An Israeli lawyer.** Any lawyer licensed in Israel.
- **The magistrate's court (בית משפט השלום).** Through its duty lawyer (עורך
  דין תורן), or at its secretariat, by the chief secretary or the deputy
  chief secretary. No appointment is needed.
- **An Israeli notary (נוטריון).** Only a notary can make a notarial
  affidavit (תצהיר נוטריוני). If you're asked for a notarial affidavit, a
  lawyer's or the court's isn't enough.
- **A foreign consul.** A consular affidavit is made at the foreign
  partner's country's embassy or consulate in Israel. It generally carries
  about the same weight as a notarial affidavit.

**Made outside Israel:** Misrad Hapnim accepts a consular affidavit made in
front of an Israeli consul abroad. It also accepts a notarial affidavit made
abroad in front of a local notary, with that country's apostille, or its
legalization where there's no apostille.

Sign it only in front of whoever confirms it, never before. You must
understand what it says: if it isn't in a language you read, have it
translated for you first. How to make one, step by step, and what it costs:
see [affidavits.md](affidavits.md).

## An exemption

Original birth certificates from the former USSR issued up to 1998 need no
authentication (5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).
