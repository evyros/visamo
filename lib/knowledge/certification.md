# Certification and translation

## Where you married

A marriage in Israel, through the rabbinate or another religious court, is
already registered: its certificate needs no certification or translation. A
certificate from another country, or from an online marriage, is certified
and, if needed, translated (below). The marriage certificate's description
has the details.

## Apostille or consular legalization

An official document from abroad has to be certified by the country that
issued it before Misrad Hapnim accepts it. It can't be certified in Israel.
The rules are in the population authority's procedure 1.3.0001 (referenced
by 5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).

### Which certification

- **A country in the Hague Apostille Convention** (the US, most of Europe
  and many others): an **apostille** from that country.
- **Any other country: consular legalization**, a chain of signatures
  (שרשרת חתימות) made in the country that issued the document (1.3.0001
  §3.1.1):
  1. the official of the office that issued it,
  2. the country's ministry of justice,
  3. its foreign ministry,
  4. the Israeli consul in that country.

  In Ethiopia, Thailand and Nepal, the ministry of justice isn't part of
  the chain (§3.1.2). Each document's guide has the steps for the countries
  it covers.
- **Outside the convention, where Israel has no embassy, though the two
  have diplomatic relations:** the last signature is from the Israeli
  embassy in a nearby country that serves it. For Tanzania, that's the Israeli embassy in
  Nairobi, Kenya; for Haiti, the Israeli embassy in the Dominican Republic.
- **Outside the convention, with no diplomatic relations with Israel:** the
  chain goes through a third country that has relations with Israel (1.3.0001
  §3.1.3): the issuing country's own signatures, then the third country's
  embassy in the issuing country, then the third country's foreign
  ministry, and last the Israeli embassy in the third country. The
  procedure's example is a document from Iran: the Iranian authority,
  Jordan's consul in Iran, Jordan's foreign ministry, and Israel's consul in
  Jordan.

### What's certified

- **The original document itself.** The apostille or legalization is on the
  original, or attached to it, and it certifies the official who signed
  that document (1.3.0001 §2.1.5). A certification of a translation, of a
  copy, or of someone's statement about the document isn't enough.
- **In the country's own language, as the office issued it.** Legalizing
  only an English version, while the original in the country's language had
  no certification, has held up a file.
- **Keep them together.** The apostille is usually attached to the
  document, and separating them voids it.
- **A document from a foreign embassy in Israel** about the foreign
  partner's details (birth, death, current or past status) needs the same
  certification and a notarized translation as one from abroad (1.3.0001
  §4.1–4.2). In practice, it doesn't replace the document from the country
  itself: a single-status declaration from the partner's embassy in Israel
  has been refused, and the office asked for the certificate from the
  country that issues it.
- **Your everyday documents need none.** A lease, bills, bank statements,
  payslips, an insurance policy and similar documents from abroad are asked
  for to show your center of life or your relationship, not as official
  records of your status: they need no apostille or legalization. Official
  documents, like a birth certificate or a police certificate, always do.

### Getting the originals

- **Bring the originals.** Misrad Hapnim doesn't accept copies of official
  documents, only the originals with their certification. A copy is
  accepted only when the original can't be had, like an old birth
  certificate.
- **Ask for a paper document "for use abroad".** Many offices reject a
  document printed at home or downloaded from an online service, and many
  countries won't put an apostille on one. Ask for the printed original, with
  a handwritten signature or the official stamp, and say it's for use abroad
  or for an apostille.
- **Some documents come only as a file.** For Israel, the Philippines
  issues its PSA certificates (birth, marriage and death certificates, and
  the certificate of no marriage, CENOMAR) only as a file with an electronic
  apostille, sent by email. Printing them makes them invalid, so there's no
  paper original to ask for or send. Misrad Hapnim usually asks for
  originals on paper, so the clerk may push back on the files: explain that
  for Israel, the Philippines issues them only this way.
- **The originals have to be in Israel** for the first appointment, even if
  the foreign partner is still abroad: send them by courier to the Israeli
  partner in time. They're also needed for the translation.
- **Order a few copies of each document.** You may need another one later,
  for a renewal, a marriage, or a document that's lost.

## Translation

What a document needs depends on two things: what kind of document it is,
and what language it's in.

### Your everyday documents and your own evidence

A lease, bills, bank statements, payslips, an insurance policy, chats,
emails, letters from friends: these need no notary.

- **In Hebrew, Arabic or English:** no translation.
- **In any other language:** translate them yourselves, or mark the
  important parts and explain them, so the clerk can follow. A notarized
  translation is needed only if the office asks for one.

The translation goes with the original, never instead of it.

### Official documents and formal forms

Certificates, court decrees, police certificates and affidavits, like the
landlord's affidavit. What they need depends on their language:

- **Hebrew or Arabic:** no translation. By law, these are the only
  languages Misrad Hapnim (משרד הפנים) accepts without one.
- **English** (a document written in English, not a translation into it):
  by law, a notarized translation into Hebrew, like any other language.
  Procedure 1.3.0001 lets a clerk accept a document in a language they
  fully understand, and in practice most clerks read English
  well enough to accept an English document without a translation,
  especially a short one. It depends on the clerk handling your file: if
  they don't accept it, they'll ask you to translate it and give you time to
  do it, so be ready to. The AS/6 checklist itself says public documents
  need a notarized translation into Hebrew "except from Arabic and English"
  (מלבד מהשפות ערבית ואנגלית), which you can point a clerk to.
- **Another language, which a notary in Israel knows** (German, Russian,
  Spanish and most others): a notarized translation (תרגום נוטריוני) into
  Hebrew, by a notary in Israel, is required (1.3.0001 §5). That's the
  requirement, not just the safest way, and it doesn't depend on the clerk.
  - **The notary (נוטריון) translates it themselves,** from the document's
    own language straight into Hebrew, and has to know both languages.
  - **Anything else will be rejected:** a translation by a translator, even
    a certified or sworn one (מתרגם מוסמך), or by a translation agency; a
    translation someone else made, which a notary only approved; a
    translation made abroad, even by a notary and with an apostille of its
    own. If you already have one of these, have the document translated
    again by a notary in Israel.
  - **Into Hebrew, not English.** A translation into English isn't what's
    asked for, and the clerk handling your file can refuse it.
  - **The translation needs no apostille.** Only the original document does.
  - **Finding a notary:** the Israel Bar Association's list of notaries
    shows the languages each one translates.
- **A language no notary in Israel knows:** the translation is made abroad.
  A local notary there translates the document into English, and that
  translation gets its own apostille, or, from a country outside the
  Apostille Convention, its own consular legalization (1.3.0001 §5.3–5.4).
  By the procedure, a notary in Israel then translates the English version
  into Hebrew: a double translation (תרגום על תרגום). In practice, the
  notarized English translation is enough, like a document written in
  English. The Hebrew translation of it is needed only if the clerk handling
  your file asks for it, because they don't read English well enough.

### The certification is translated too

The translation covers the document and its apostille or legalization. An
apostille that's in English, or that includes English, usually needs no
translation.

## Affidavits

An affidavit (תצהיר) is a written statement that a person (the affiant,
המצהיר) makes of their own free will. They swear or affirm that it's true, in
front of someone the law authorizes to confirm it, who checks who they are
and warns them that they must tell the truth, and that giving false
information is punishable by law. Signing one is a legal act. In the partner
visa process, an affidavit proves a fact no official document shows: for
example, when the foreign partner's country doesn't issue a certain
certificate.

An affidavit is either **written freely**, as a list of numbered statements
with a section at the bottom for its confirmation (אישור), or **part of an
official form**, where you only fill in the missing details. Form AS/6 has
affidavits like this; they're explained with the form.

**Who can confirm one in Israel:**

- **An Israeli lawyer.** Any lawyer licensed in Israel.
- **The magistrate's court (בית משפט השלום).** Through its duty lawyer (עורך
  דין תורן), or at its secretariat, by the chief secretary or the deputy
  chief secretary. No appointment is needed.
- **An Israeli notary (נוטריון).** Only a notary can make a notarial
  affidavit (תצהיר נוטריוני). If you're asked for a notarial affidavit, a
  lawyer's or the court's isn't enough.
- **A foreign consul.** A consular affidavit is made at the foreign
  partner's country's embassy or consulate in Israel. It generally carries
  about the same weight as a notarial affidavit.

**Made outside Israel:** Misrad Hapnim accepts a consular affidavit made in
front of an Israeli consul abroad. It also accepts a notarial affidavit made
abroad in front of a local notary, with that country's apostille, or its
legalization where there's no apostille.

Sign it only in front of whoever confirms it, never before. You must
understand what it says: if it isn't in a language you read, have it
translated for you first. How to make one, step by step, and what it costs:
see [affidavits.md](affidavits.md).

## An exemption

Original birth certificates from the former USSR issued up to 1998 need no
authentication (5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).
