# Certification and translation

## Where you married

A marriage in Israel, through the rabbinate or another religious court, is
already registered: its certificate needs no certification or translation. A
certificate from another country, or from an online marriage, is certified
and, if needed, translated (below). The marriage certificate's description
has the details.

## Apostille or consular legalization

A document from abroad has to be certified before Israel accepts it. Only
the country that issued it can certify it: it can't be done in Israel.

- From a country in the **Hague Apostille Convention** (the US, most of
  Europe and many others): an **apostille** from that country.
- From any other country: **consular legalization**, a longer chain of
  signatures (שרשרת חתימות): the authority that issued the document, then
  usually the country's ministry of justice and its foreign ministry, and last the
  Israeli consulate there.

Foreign documents follow the population authority's procedure 1.3.0001
(referenced by 5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).

- **Bring the originals.** Misrad Hapnim doesn't accept copies of these
  documents, only the originals with their certification. A copy is accepted
  only when the original can't be had, like an old birth certificate.
- **The originals have to be in Israel** for the first appointment, even if
  the foreign partner is still abroad: send them by courier to the Israeli
  partner in time. They're also needed for the translation.
- **Ask for a paper document "for use abroad".** Many offices reject a
  document printed at home or downloaded from an online service, and many
  countries won't put an apostille on one. Ask for the printed original, with
  a handwritten signature or the official stamp, and say it's for use abroad
  or for an apostille.
- **Don't separate the apostille from the document.** It's usually attached
  to it, and separating them voids it.
- **Order a few copies of each document.** You may need another one later,
  for a renewal, a marriage, or a document that's lost.

## Translation

- **By law, Misrad Hapnim (משרד הפנים) accepts only Hebrew and Arabic without a
  translation.** A document in any other language needs a notarized
  translation (תרגום נוטריוני). Procedure 1.3.0001 also lets a clerk accept a
  document in a language they fully understand.
- **English, in practice:** most clerks read English well enough to accept an
  English document without a translation, especially a short one. It depends
  on the clerk handling your file. If they don't accept it, they'll ask you
  to translate it and give you time to do it. So with English documents, be
  ready to translate.
  The AS/6 checklist itself says public documents need a notarized
  translation into Hebrew "except from Arabic and English" (מלבד מהשפות
  ערבית ואנגלית), which you can point a clerk to.
- **What's translated:** the document, and the apostille or legalization on
  it too. An apostille that's in English, or that includes English, usually
  needs no translation.
- **Who translates:** a notary who knows both languages and translates it
  themselves. A translation someone else made, which a notary only
  approved, isn't accepted. Translate from the document's own language
  straight into Hebrew.
- **Translate into Hebrew, not English.** When a document needs a
  translation, a translation into English is a risk: the clerk handling your
  file may not read English well enough to accept it. Hebrew is the safest
  choice, and makes for a smoother process.
- **If no notary in Israel knows the language,** Misrad Hapnim allows a double
  translation: first into a third language abroad (English, say), then from
  it into Hebrew in Israel.
- **We recommend translating in Israel.** A translation done in Israel needs
  no apostille: only the original document does. A notarized translation done
  abroad needs an apostille of its own, from the country where it was made,
  and is sometimes not accepted for other reasons. So a document translated
  abroad has two apostilles: one on the document, one on the translation.
- **Your own evidence** (chats, emails, letters from friends): it doesn't need
  a notary. If it isn't in Hebrew or English, translate it yourselves, or mark
  the important parts and explain them, so the clerk can follow.

## Affidavits

An affidavit (תצהיר) is a written statement you sign in front of someone the
law authorizes to confirm it, after they warn you to tell the truth. It's
how you prove a fact no official document shows, for example when a country
doesn't issue a civil-status certificate.

- **In Israel,** any Israeli lawyer can confirm one. So can the magistrate's
  court (בית משפט השלום), at its secretariat or through its duty lawyer,
  without an appointment, for a small fixed fee.
- **A notarial affidavit** (תצהיר נוטריוני) is made only in front of a notary.
  If you're asked for one, a lawyer's isn't enough.
- **A consular affidavit** is made at a consulate: the foreign partner's
  country's consulate in Israel, or an Israeli consulate abroad.
- **Made abroad, in front of a local notary:** accepted, with that country's
  apostille or legalization.
- Sign it only in front of whoever confirms it, never before. You must
  understand what it says: if it isn't in a language you read, have it
  translated for you first.
- The affidavits inside Misrad Hapnim's forms (AS/6) are explained with each
  form.

## An exemption

Original birth certificates from the former USSR issued up to 1998 need no
authentication (5.2.0008 §ד.2.ה).
